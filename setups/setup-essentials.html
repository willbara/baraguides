    Essentials Setup

    last updated 2026-11-05 · 7 changes · one afternoon · what changed

    Essentials is sane defaults and quick, big wins: a browser that blocks trackers, login codes that stop a stolen password from being enough, a password manager, and automatic updates. Who this is for: anyone starting from zero. The risks here are data brokers, ad networks, and everyday criminals, who target everybody. Nothing on this page asks you to leave a platform or explain yourself to family.

    four habits behind the setup

    Be mindful of apps and settings

    Every app you install asks for access to something, and its defaults favor the company. Install fewer apps, look at the permissions and privacy settings when you install, and check them again now and then, because apps add features and change defaults.

    Know how a free service pays

    If you can't see how a free service makes money, your data is probably how. Before you sign up, ask what the company gets from you.

    Share the minimum

    Give each service only what it needs. A real birthday, phone number, or address is rarely required. Assume anything you post or type into a form can be copied and kept.

    Slow down on urgent messages

    Most scams run on urgency. Go to the site yourself instead of clicking a link, and never hand over a login code or password because someone asked.

    the plan Seven changes, ordered by how much protection each one gives for the effort. Start at the top. If the afternoon runs out, stop after any step and you are still better off than this morning.

    the setup

    Automatic updates

    updates
    ~15 minevery device

    The job: Updates close security holes that attackers already know about. Most break-ins use a fix that has existed for months.

    Why this one: Turning on automatic updates protects you more than any app on this site, and it costs nothing.

    1. Turn on automatic updates for your phone, your computer, your app stores, and your browser.
    2. Delete apps you no longer use. Every app is another way in, and most phones carry twenty forgotten ones.
    3. Check which apps can see your location, microphone, and contacts. Revoke what they don't need. An app asks again if it truly does.
    no product to pick just a setting

    Bitwarden

    password manager
    ~45 minstart with three passwords

    The job: A password manager creates and remembers a different password for every account. It replaces reusing one password everywhere, which is how a single leak turns into ten break-ins.

    Why Bitwarden: Its free plan syncs across all your devices, and it has had independent security audits.

    Look for: A plan you can afford that syncs across your devices, independent audits, and an export so you can leave. Backup: KeePassXC if you want your vault stored only on your own devices.

    1. Create the account with a long passphrase: four random words you can remember. Write it on paper for now.
    2. Install the browser extension and the phone app, and sign in to both.
    3. Change your email, banking, and most-used social account to generated passwords. Move the rest as you log in over the next month.

    Ente Auth

    login codes
    ~30 minemail first

    The job: A two-factor (2FA) app gives you a short-lived code to type after your password. It replaces text-message codes, which can be intercepted, and a stolen password stops being enough to take your account.

    Why Ente Auth: It backs up your codes with encryption, so a lost phone does not lock you out, and it runs on phones and computers.

    Look for: An encrypted backup or export of your codes, and an app for every device you use. Backup: Aegis if you only use Android.

    1. Install it. Turn on 2FA for your email first, then banking, then your password manager.
    2. Each site gives you recovery codes. Keep them in your password manager. The password manager's own codes go on paper.
    3. Once the app works, remove text-message codes where the site lets you.

    Brave

    browser
    ~20 minnothing to add on top

    The job: Your browser is where most everyday tracking happens. A tracker-blocking browser replaces one that lets ad networks follow you from site to site.

    Why Brave: It blocks trackers and ads out of the box, so there is nothing to install on top.

    Look for: Tracker and ad blocking that is on by default, and regular security updates. Backup: Firefox if you want a browser that is not built on Chromium (the engine behind Chrome).

    1. Install it and import your bookmarks and passwords. Then move the passwords into your password manager.
    2. In settings, turn off Rewards, Wallet, and News. Leave the blocking (called Shields) on its defaults.
    3. Keep the default search engine for a week before you judge it.
    features, trade-offs, and alternatives Browsers & Search page →

    Signal

    messenger
    ~15 minplus some persuasion

    The job: A private messenger scrambles your messages so only you and the person you write to can read them (end-to-end encryption). It replaces texting and chat apps where the company can read along.

    Why Signal: It feels like a normal messenger, so it is the private tool you can realistically get other people onto.

    Look for: Encryption that is always on, no ads, and an app that collects little about you. Backup: SimpleX if you don't want to give out a phone number.

    1. Install it, register, and set a PIN you will remember.
    2. Set a username and share that instead of your phone number.
    3. Turn on a default disappearing-messages timer. Then move one group chat over. The family one is usually easiest.
    features, trade-offs, and alternatives Messengers page →

    AdGuard DNS

    encrypted DNS
    ~15 minno account

    The job: DNS is the lookup that turns a website name into an address, and your internet provider normally sees every one. An encrypted DNS service hides those lookups from the network and blocks known ad, tracker, and malware domains.

    Why AdGuard DNS: It needs no account, one setting covers a whole device, and there is nothing to maintain.

    Look for: Support for encrypted lookups, a blocklist, and a clear statement about what it logs. Backup: Quad9 if you want malware blocking without ad blocking.

    1. On your phone, set encrypted DNS to AdGuard's address. adguard-dns.io has the exact steps for Android and iPhone.
    2. Do the same on your computer or inside your browser's settings.
    3. Optional: set it on your router to cover the TV and everything else. Most routers send lookups unencrypted, so treat this as a bonus.
    addresses, prices, and alternatives DNS & Network page →

    Proton Mail

    email
    ~20 minkeep your old email for now

    The job: Your email is the reset address for every other account, so whoever reads it can take over the rest. A provider that cannot read your mail replaces an inbox the provider can.

    Why Proton Mail: Its free plan gives you one encrypted address with no payment card, and the Standard setup builds on the same account.

    Look for: End-to-end encryption, a free or cheap plan, and support for your own domain later. Backup: Mailbox.org if you want to use any mail app you like.

    1. Sign up for the free account and install the phone app.
    2. Use the new address for new signups and anything sensitive. Leave your old email running.
    3. Add the new address as a second recovery address on your important accounts, next to the old one.

    after setup

    Let the password move happen on its own. Each time you log in somewhere over the next month, save it to your password manager and replace the password if you reused it. Forcing it all in one sitting is how people burn out.

    Retire the old browser after two weeks. Keep it as a fallback while the new one proves itself, then uninstall it so you don't drift back.

    Watch for this mistake: 2FA without recovery codes. Lockouts hurt more than hackers at this level. If you skipped the recovery codes in the login codes step, go back and save them.
