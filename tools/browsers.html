    Browsers & Search

    last updated 2026-11-05 · 7 recommendations · what changed

    Your browser sees everything you do online, which makes it the single biggest privacy decision on a normal computer. The good news: the right pick works out of the box. No extension pile, no settings safari.

    before you pick More hardening is not automatically better. Aggressive fingerprinting protection breaks sites, and a heavily customized browser can be more identifiable, not less. Pick the tier of friction you'll actually live with: a private browser you abandon after a week protects nothing.

    what actually matters

    tracking protection by default

    It blocks trackers and third-party cookies out of the box, not buried behind flags or an extension you have to know to install.

    fingerprinting resistance

    Trackers don't need cookies if your browser's configuration is unique. Resisting that matters more every year.

    engine security

    Chromium is more battle-tested and extensively vetted than Gecko: a flat security reality that privacy circles often wave away. The monoculture concern is real too; it's just a different axis.

    update cadence

    Browsers are the most attacked software you run. Fast security updates beat any privacy tweak.

    plain-english key

    browser engine

    The part of a browser that loads and draws web pages. Chromium powers Chrome, Brave, Vivaldi, and Helium. Gecko powers Firefox, LibreWolf, and Mullvad Browser. WebKit powers Safari and Orion.

    fingerprinting

    Sites can recognize you from your browser's setup, such as screen size, fonts, and graphics card, without any cookie. Defenses either randomize those details or make every user look the same.

    Manifest V2 and V3

    The rules for how browser extensions work. Google's newer rules (V3) limit what ad blockers can do, and Chrome has dropped the older ones (V2). A few Chromium browsers still keep V2 extensions alive.

    uBlock Origin

    A free, open-source extension that blocks ads and trackers. Its full version needs the older V2 rules.

    search index

    A search engine's own copy of the web, built by crawling sites itself. Many "private" engines borrow results from Google or Bing instead of keeping one.

    recommendations

    Brave

    the default pick
    chromiumopen sourcead blockinganti-fingerprintingfree

    Brave is a direct Chromium derivative (not a fork), which keeps it close to upstream and gives it a security edge over Gecko-based alternatives. It ships with aggressive ad and tracker blocking, one of the most thorough anti-fingerprinting implementations in any mainstream browser, and a shields system that works without a separate extension layer. The BAT crypto rewards it's known for are entirely opt-in, and you can switch them off in under a minute.

    Brave Origin is a paid variant that removes features not essential to privacy or security, including the Leo AI assistant, Rewards, Wallet, VPN, and Tor, plus usage pings and crash logs. The standalone desktop build leaves them out entirely. On Android, iOS, or the regular desktop app, Origin is an upgrade that turns them off. It's a one-time ~$59.99 and free on Linux. Origin checks your purchase with blind tokens (Privacy Pass), which Brave says never link your device to your account.

    good
    • Strong blocking and fingerprint randomization with zero setup
    • Chromium's security posture and site compatibility
    • Hosts uBlock Origin and three other Manifest V2 extensions
    • Origin variant: minimalist build, free on Linux
    • Brave Search built in, on an independent index
    mind the
    • Chromium monoculture is a systemic concern
    • Rewards and wallet clutter in the standard build
    • Origin's licence check adds one more trust layer
    free · Origin ~$59.99 one-off (free on linux) brave.com →

    Firefox

    the gecko pick
    geckoopen sourcefree

    Firefox is still a valid pick for people who genuinely need Gecko: the full extension ecosystem (including unrestricted uBlock Origin), certain enterprise compatibility, or an ideological preference against the Chromium monoculture. Be honest about the work, though: out of the box it isn't competitive on privacy. You'll need to harden it (arkenfox user.js or a pre-hardened build like LibreWolf) and switch off telemetry and sponsored content.

    Mozilla is also adding AI features. Since Firefox 148 (February 2026), a "Block AI enhancements" switch in Settings turns off all current and future generative AI features at once. Smart Window, an AI browsing mode, reached the US, Canada, and France in Firefox 155 (September 2026). It is opt-in and needs a Mozilla account.

    good
    • Gecko is the main non-Chromium engine on Windows and Linux
    • Unrestricted extensions, including full uBlock Origin
    • Deeply configurable for those willing to do the work
    • One switch turns off every AI feature
    mind the
    • Requires hardening to match what Brave does by default
    • Telemetry and sponsored tiles ship enabled
    • Gecko gets less security scrutiny than Chromium
    • Funding still mostly comes from Google's search deal
    free mozilla.org →

    Helium

    the one to watch
    chromiumopen sourcead blockingno accountfree

    Helium is a lean open-source Chromium browser built on ungoogled-chromium (Chromium with Google's services removed) by a small team called imput, which funds it through user sponsorships. The privacy defaults are strong: trackers and third-party cookies are blocked out of the box by its own build of uBlock Origin, and the attack surface is kept small by deliberately omitting sync, a built-in password manager, and DRM (the copy protection streaming sites use). It runs on macOS, Windows, and Linux only. It's still in beta, so it's not a daily-driver replacement yet, but it's genuinely interesting for purists who want even less than Brave Origin.

    good
    • Privacy by omission: less shipped means less to audit and leak
    • Google services removed at the ungoogled-chromium level
    • Bundles its own uBlock Origin build, no account needed
    • Funded by sponsors, with no monetization angle
    • Follows Chromium releases within about two days
    mind the
    • Beta software from a small team
    • Desktop only: no Android or iOS version
    • No DRM means no Netflix, and no sync means you're the sync
    free helium.computer →

    Mullvad Browser

    recommended secondary
    geckoanti-fingerprintingopen sourcefree

    Mullvad and the Tor Project (the group behind Tor Browser) develop this browser together, using the same hardened Firefox base as Tor Browser but without the Tor network attached. The strategy is different from most "private browser" pitches: it aims to make you look identical to every other Mullvad Browser user, because a shared, locked-down fingerprint fights tracking far better than per-user randomization. It pairs naturally with Mullvad VPN, but it works perfectly well with no VPN at all. Treat it as a secondary for sensitive browsing instead of a daily driver.

    In October 2026, the Tor Project said it is keeping its technical work with Mullvad but pausing joint promotion. This followed criticism of a personal donation by a Mullvad co-owner to a Swedish political party. Tor also reworded how it describes the browser on its own channels. The browser is still updated, and version 16.0 came out on 29 October 2026.

    good
    • Uniform fingerprint by design: strong defense against tracking, not just blocking
    • Built and maintained by two organizations with real anti-surveillance track records
    • Doesn't require Mullvad VPN or any subscription
    mind the
    • Aggressive hardening breaks some sites, expect occasional friction
    • Almost no extensions, by design
    • Best as a secondary browser for sensitive browsing
    • Tor paused joint promotion with Mullvad in October 2026
    free mullvad.net/browser →

    Vivaldi

    the power-user pick
    chromiumad blockingcustomizablefree

    Vivaldi is a Chromium browser built around customization first, privacy second: tab stacking, panels, a built-in mail client, and more interface options than almost anything else out there. It ships built-in tracker and ad blocking, which puts it ahead of stock Chromium and the many forks that ship nothing, but it's more feature-dense and mainstream than privacy-first. It's a reasonable pick if you want Chromium's compatibility, a genuinely useful blocker, and lots of customization.

    Vivaldi 8.3 (5 November 2026) rebuilt that blocker. It now has four levels, from off to aggressive. You can set a level for all sites or one site at a time, and you can add your own filter lists. Vivaldi has also said it won't add an AI chatbot or page summaries to the browser (August 2025).

    good
    • Built-in tracker and ad blocking, with per-site levels
    • Extremely configurable UI: tab stacking, panels, custom shortcuts
    • Chromium compatibility and update cadence
    • No built-in AI chatbot or page summaries
    • No Google account ties; independent Norwegian company
    mind the
    • Feature-dense by design: more surface area than a minimalist browser
    • Fingerprinting resistance is weaker than Brave or Mullvad Browser
    • Still Chromium under the hood, with the same monoculture trade-off
    • Sends a daily usage ping with a unique install ID
    free vivaldi.com →

    LibreWolf

    secondary, research-grade
    geckoopen sourcefree

    LibreWolf is Firefox with arkenfox-style hardening (a well-known set of privacy settings) baked in before you ever open it: aggressive fingerprinting resistance, telemetry stripped, trackers blocked by default. The honest take: this is not recommended as a daily driver. The hardening that makes it strong also breaks sites more often than most people will put up with day to day. Updates have closed the gap with Firefox proper (patches usually land within three days of upstream), though the structural risk remains: a smaller team with no centralized telemetry feedback loop. It's better used as a secondary, research-grade browser than an everyday one.

    Updating depends on your system. LibreWolf updates itself on macOS. On Windows, the installer offers an optional scheduled updater (added in April 2026). On Linux, you update through your package manager.

    good
    • Aggressive fingerprinting resistance and tracker blocking out of the box
    • Telemetry and sponsored content stripped before first launch
    • Patches usually arrive within three days of Firefox
    mind the
    • Updates are optional on Windows and manual on Linux
    • Safe Browsing (the malware and phishing blocklist) is off by default
    • Inherits Gecko's weaker sandboxing and site isolation versus Chromium
    • Small volunteer team, no staffed security org or telemetry feedback loop
    free librewolf.net →

    Orion

    the webkit pick
    🇺🇸 usa🍎 apple onlywebkitclosed sourcefree

    Orion is a WebKit browser from Kagi, the paid search engine, built by a small team and funded by its users instead of ads. Its content blocker is the main draw on both macOS and iOS: EasyList, EasyPrivacy, and AdGuard's base list run by default, and you can add any Adblock Plus-style filter list or type in custom rules of your own. It's highly customizable too, with vertical and compact tabs, profiles, and programmable buttons, and it runs Chrome and Firefox extensions on Safari's engine, so it's the pick if you want WebKit with real control over blocking. Kagi says it collects no usage telemetry. The platform story matters just as much, though. Kagi ended development of the Linux and Windows versions on 2 October 2026, so Orion is now a macOS and iOS browser, and the Linux beta has stopped receiving updates. Kagi has promised to release the Linux and Windows code, but no release had been confirmed as of November 2026.

    good
    • Blocking compiles to native WebKit content-blocking rules, which Kagi says has minimal performance impact
    • Orion 1.1 (June 2026) added containers and built-in translation
    • Crash reports are opt-in, and update checks send only the browser version without logging your IP, according to its privacy policy
    • Kagi invites you to test the no-telemetry claim yourself with a traffic proxy such as mitmproxy
    mind the
    • The blocker can't filter URL parameters (even with uBlock Origin) or run snippet filters, so it covers less than uBlock Origin on Firefox
    • Only some components are public, so you can't read the browser's code
    • No third-party audit has confirmed the telemetry claim, and nobody has published fingerprinting-resistance tests
    • It inherits WebKit's vulnerabilities, and nobody has measured how fast Orion ships patches next to Safari
    • Sync works only between Apple devices
    free · optional Orion+ $5/mo, $50/yr, or $150 lifetime orionbrowser.com →

    search engines

    Brave Search as primary. It runs an independent index (not a reskinned Bing or Google feed), which puts it in a different category from most "private" search engines. Result quality is competitive and well past the rising-star phase. It's the default in Brave already; in other browsers it's two clicks to set. Free results include privacy-respecting paid ads, and a Premium plan removes them. Publishers are also suing Brave over its search index and AI answers: News Corp in July 2026, and the New York Times and three other newspapers on 2 November 2026. Neither case is decided.

    Startpage as secondary. You get proxied Google results plus the Anonymous View feature: the pragmatic answer when you need Google's result quality without the direct relationship. The caveat: it's owned by System1, an ad-tech company. It's fine as a secondary with clear utility, but not trustworthy enough by design to be your primary.

    Qwant if you want a second independent index. It's French, sits under GDPR, and has a credible privacy stance. It builds a partial index of its own with Ecosia, in a joint venture called EUSP that has served results since August 2025, and it still draws some results from other providers. France's data regulator (CNIL) ruled in February 2025 that data Qwant once sent to Microsoft for ads was pseudonymous, not anonymous as Qwant had said. It issued a reminder with no fine, and Qwant had already corrected its policy in 2020. Qwant isn't as thorough as Brave Search, but it's a reasonable European-backed option to rotate in.

    DuckDuckGo, with eyes open. It's usable and easy, but it's primarily Bing-backed and inherits Bing's limitations. In 2022 its CEO publicly endorsed down-ranking sites associated with Russian disinformation; much of the mechanism came from Microsoft's own policy changes, but the endorsement was DDG's. If you can't control your index, you can't fully control your results. It's fine for casual use; just don't treat it as neutral.

    Kagi if you'll pay to opt out of the ad economy entirely. It's a paid, no-ads, no-tracking search engine with independent ranking signals: your subscription funds the business directly instead of your data or attention doing it indirectly. It pulls results from other search engines and its own small index, then ranks them itself. Plans start at $5 a month for 300 searches, and $10 gets unlimited searches. You can try 50 searches without an account. It's worth it if search quality is something you'll budget for like any other tool, instead of something that has to be free.

    SearXNG for a self-hostable, no-account option. It's an open-source metasearch engine that pulls results from other engines (Google, Bing, Brave Search, and more) without forwarding your identity to any of them. Run your own instance for full control, or use one of the many public instances if you trust the operator. SearXNG's own docs say you can't tell whether a public instance logs your searches.

    worth knowing

    Fingerprinting matters more than cookies now. Trackers don't need a cookie if your screen size, fonts, GPU, and timezone combine into a configuration unique enough to identify you across sites. Aggressive fingerprinting defenses (Brave's randomization, Mullvad Browser's uniformity) address this directly; stock Chromium and unhardened Firefox don't.

    uBlock Origin is the one extension worth installing. Every extra "privacy" extension piled on top tends to make you more identifiable, not less: each one adds another fingerprintable signature to your browser's configuration. On Brave, Shields already cover what most of those extensions claim to do, so skip the pile entirely. On Firefox, Gecko still runs uBlock Origin unrestricted, unlike Chromium's Manifest V3 limits. Brave still hosts its own copy of the full version, and Helium bundles its own build. Brave calls its support best-effort.

    Tor Browser is what gives you actual anonymity, not these. Nothing else on this page hides who you are from a determined observer: these picks reduce tracking; they don't make you anonymous. Private or incognito mode in particular isn't privacy: it only clears local history, while your ISP, employer, DNS resolver, and the sites themselves see exactly the same traffic. It's a different problem that needs a different tool.
