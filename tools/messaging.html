    Messengers

    last updated 2026-11-04 · 7 recommendations · what changed

    Before picking a messenger, frame it as platform replacement: what you're leaving matters as much as what you're joining. SMS to iMessage is a meaningful gain. WhatsApp to Signal is a significant one. WhatsApp to Telegram isn't an upgrade at all. It's a lateral move into something arguably worse, wearing a privacy costume.

    before you pick End-to-end encryption protects message content, not the fact that you messaged someone, when, or how often. That's metadata, and it's where messengers really differ. Who can see your social graph matters as much as who can read your texts.

    what actually matters

    e2ee by default

    Encryption you have to remember to turn on is encryption that's usually off. This single criterion disqualifies more apps than any other.

    metadata exposure

    What the server learns: your contacts, group memberships, timing patterns. The best designs can't even see who's talking to whom.

    identifier required

    Needing a phone number ties chats to a real-world identity. Usernames are better; no identifier at all is the frontier.

    protocol scrutiny

    Open, publicly reviewed encryption designs (Signal's, and a newer standard called MLS) have survived years of expert attention. Custom in-house designs haven't earned that trust.

    plain-english key

    end-to-end encryption (e2ee)

    Only you and the person you're messaging can read a message. Not the app's company, and not a hacker who breaks into its servers.

    metadata

    Everything about a message except its content: who you talk to, when, how often, and from where.

    forward secrecy

    Encryption keys that change constantly, so someone who steals one today can't unlock your old messages.

    post-quantum

    Encryption built to survive future quantum computers, which could break today's. Attackers can record scrambled messages now and try to crack them later.

    federated

    Run by many independent servers that talk to each other, like email, instead of one company's.

    onion routing

    Messages hop through several relays, each knowing only the next hop, so none sees both sender and recipient. Tor is the best-known example.

    server-side encryption

    The company encrypts your chats but holds the keys, so it can still read them. That's not the same as end-to-end.

    audit

    An independent security firm combs through an app's code and design, then publishes what it found.

    recommendations

    Signal

    the default pick
    🇺🇸 usae2ee alwaysmetadata-resistantopen sourcefree

    Signal is the current gold standard, and not just by reputation. Its encryption design, the Signal Protocol, has been studied by cryptographers more than any other consumer messenger's and holds up. Everyone else borrows it. It's also already built to withstand future quantum computers when a chat starts, with the same protection rolling out to ongoing messages. It keeps almost no metadata (subpoena responses have famously contained just two timestamps), hides the sender from Signal's own servers (called sealed sender), and has disappearing messages built in. It also looks like a normal app, which is why you can actually get your group chats to move here. Use it for anything sensitive, and it's reliable on every platform for everything else.

    On Android, Molly is a hardened copy of Signal that encrypts the messages stored on your phone and works with Tor through the Orbot app. It uses the same network and contacts, but its last release was 31 July 2026 and it now trails Signal's own updates, so check that it's current before relying on it.

    good
    • End-to-end encrypts everything: messages, calls, groups, attachments
    • Servers learn very little about who you talk to
    • Usernames let you chat without sharing your number
    • Encrypted backups move your history to a new phone
    • Automatic key verification catches swapped contact keys
    • Nonprofit, open source, and heavily audited
    mind the
    • Phone number still required to register
    • Centralized: one service, one US-based operator
    • Desktop app must be linked to a phone first
    • Free backups cover 45 days of media; up to 100 GB is paid
    • Two 2026 research attacks needed a compromised Signal server; both are patched
    free, donation-funded signal.org →

    iMessage

    the apple-baseline pick
    🍎 apple onlye2eeclosed sourcefree

    iMessage is a reasonable secondary for everyday conversation between Apple devices: messages are end-to-end encrypted (E2EE, so only you and the recipient can read them), there's zero setup, and it's a real step up from SMS or unencrypted RCS. The trust model is simple: you're trusting Apple entirely. Since iOS 26.5, green-bubble chats with Android can be E2EE over RCS (the modern replacement for SMS) too. It's still beta, though, the Android side has to use Google Messages, and both carriers have to support it (AT&T, T-Mobile, and Verizon are all on Apple's list), so you'll fall back to unencrypted RCS or SMS when it isn't available. iCloud backups are a separate trap: they're not E2EE until you turn on Advanced Data Protection, an iCloud setting that encrypts your backups so Apple can't read them. A useful way to think about it: Signal for anything sensitive, iMessage as the baseline for people who won't install Signal.

    good
    • End-to-end encrypted by default between Apple devices, no setup
    • Big upgrade over SMS and RCS
    • Built to withstand future quantum computers since iOS 17.4
    • Already on the phones your family uses
    • Encrypted RCS now reaches some Android contacts
    mind the
    • iCloud backups aren't end-to-end encrypted unless you opt in, and UK users can't
    • Encrypted RCS with Android is still beta and partial
    • Closed source and Apple-only
    free with apple hardware apple ADP guide →

    SimpleX

    the no-identifier pick
    no identifier requirede2ee alwaysopen sourcemetadata-resistantfree

    SimpleX is genuinely interesting if you face serious surveillance: there are no user IDs whatsoever, not even phone numbers or usernames. Messages pass through one-way relay queues (temporary mailboxes on servers) that nobody can piece together into a map of who talks to whom, so the privacy comes from the design instead of a promise. It's newer and less battle-tested than Signal, but it's the most privacy-forward design you can get in a consumer messenger right now.

    Since April 2026 it also has public channels (launched in beta) for broadcasting to an audience. The company behind it is for-profit, opened a crowdfunding round in August 2026, and is rolling out two optional paid extras: supporter badges (larger files, longer storage) and public names, whose sale starts December 12. SimpleX says neither adds a user identifier.

    good
    • No identifier means nothing to subpoena, leak, or piece together
    • Privacy comes from the design, not a promise
    • Open source, with relays you can host yourself
    • Independently audited twice (Trail of Bits)
    • Built to withstand future quantum computers
    mind the
    • Shorter audit history than Signal's; a third audit is still unpublished
    • Multi-device use and delivery still have rough edges
    • Small network: you'll be onboarding contacts yourself
    • For-profit company that is now crowdfunding
    free, open source simplex.chat →

    Matrix / Element

    the federation pick
    federatede2ee optionalself-hostableopen sourcefree

    Matrix is a protocol (a shared set of rules) instead of a company: like email, anyone can run a server and talk to every other server, and Element is the most popular app for using it. That makes it messaging nobody can switch off, and the right answer for communities, team chat, and people who want to own their infrastructure. Know the fine print, though. End-to-end encryption is available but isn't always on by default (it depends on how the server and app are set up), and every server in a room can see that room's metadata. Expect more friction than Signal, since this is a different use case, not a replacement. Element X is the app to start with, because Element plans to retire the older Element Classic. Element also plans to block unverified devices from encrypted chats, but it postponed that in September 2026. If you'd rather host your own server, Element's free Server Suite Community edition is built for small, non-professional setups.

    good
    • No central operator: run your own server or pick any
    • Encrypted DMs and private rooms (check your app's defaults)
    • Rich rooms, threads, and bridges to other messengers
    • No phone number needed
    mind the
    • Encryption isn't guaranteed; it depends on setup
    • Every server in a room sees its metadata
    • Device verification and key management confuse newcomers
    free · self-hosting only costs you a server element.io →

    Briar

    the off-grid pick
    peer-to-peeronion-routedworks offlineopen sourcefree

    Briar has no server to subpoena because there is no server: messages go straight between phones, either over Tor (a network that hides where your traffic comes from) or over Bluetooth and local Wi-Fi when the internet is down or blocked. It's built for activists and journalists under real pressure. As a daily messenger it's spartan; as a tool of last resort it's one of a kind. The catch is that Briar went into maintenance mode in July 2026. The team decided to shut it down in 2025 because it had no funding and only spare-time developers, then reversed course. Now it gets security fixes and bug fixes but no new features, so treat it as a finished tool instead of one that's improving.

    good
    • No central anything: nothing to block, seize, or log
    • Works without internet over Bluetooth or Wi-Fi
    • Traffic goes over Tor, with no phone number or email
    • Desktop beta for Windows, macOS, and Linux
    mind the
    • No iPhone version, and the desktop beta lacks offline features
    • Both people must be online to exchange messages
    • No calls, account backup, or file attachments
    • Drains battery and runs unreliably in the background on Android
    • Maintenance mode: bug and security fixes only
    free briarproject.org →

    Threema

    the pay-once pick
    🇨🇭 switzerlandno phone numberone-time purchasepartially open source

    Threema skips subscriptions entirely: you pay once, get a randomly generated Threema ID, and you're done. It doesn't require a phone number or email. It's under Swiss jurisdiction and has focused on keeping metadata minimal for years, so the server learns very little about who's talking to whom. Most of the app code is open source and independently audited, though parts of the backend stay closed. Ownership changed in January 2026 to Comitis Capital, a German private-equity firm (an investment company that buys businesses). That's Threema's second private-equity owner since 2020. Threema also announced a partnership with IBM Research in early 2026 to make its encryption withstand future quantum computers, though it hasn't set a release date.

    good
    • No phone number or email, just a random ID
    • One-time purchase, not a subscription
    • Swiss jurisdiction and minimal metadata by design
    • Independently audited; most code is open source
    mind the
    • Smaller network than Signal's
    • Some server-side code isn't open source
    • Owned by a German private-equity firm since January 2026
    • One-time cost rose to €6
    • Disrupted by DDoS (traffic-flood) attacks in August 2026; no data compromised
    €6 one-time, comitis capital (de) threema.ch →

    Session

    the onion-routed pick
    onion-routedno phone numberdecentralizedopen sourcefree

    Session sends every message through a network of community-run service nodes instead of a company-run server. Each node passes the message along so that none sees both sender and recipient, which means there's no central operator to subpoena for your metadata. The Session Technology Foundation (formerly the team behind Oxen) builds it, and you don't need a phone number or email, since an account is just a generated ID.

    The bigger gap is that Session doesn't have forward secrecy yet, so a stolen account key could expose past messages. A v2 protocol that adds it, along with protection against future quantum computers, is designed but hasn't shipped. Two independent research papers in 2026 also found flaws in its design, including weak checks that each person is who they claim to be, and Session hasn't publicly confirmed fixes. A 2026 funding crisis nearly shut the project down too. It now runs on donations with two or three developers plus volunteers, down from about twelve full-time.

    good
    • No phone number, email, or other identifier required
    • No central server to compel; community-run nodes relay messages
    • Open source apps and protocol
    mind the
    • 2026 research found design flaws, including a possible network takeover
    • No forward secrecy or quantum-resistant encryption yet
    • Small team after the 2026 funding crisis
    • Onion routing trades some speed for privacy
    • Small user base: you'll be onboarding contacts yourself
    free, open source getsession.org →

    Telegram

    the one to avoid
    e2ee not defaultsecret chats onlycustom mtprotocloud-stored historyfree

    Telegram is not a private messenger, and that needs saying clearly because it gets mislabeled as one constantly. Regular chats and all group chats are encrypted only between you and Telegram's servers (server-side encryption), so Telegram holds the keys and can read them. Secret Chats are the one exception, and they're nobody's default. The company doesn't hold the line under pressure either. France arrested the CEO in 2024 and got policy shifts, not principled resistance. Russia charged him with facilitating terrorism in July 2026. Apple briefly pulled the app worldwide in August 2026 after scanning it and finding illegal content. It's a cloud messenger with a privacy reputation it hasn't earned. Treat it as social media, never as a Signal replacement.

    good
    • Huge user base worldwide, plus channels and bots
    • Big file limits and chats that sync across devices
    • Familiar, polished UX
    mind the
    • Telegram holds the decryption keys for regular and group chats, so they aren't true end-to-end encryption
    • Secret Chats are end-to-end encrypted but off by default, and missing on Windows and Linux desktop
    • Custom MTProto encryption, far less examined than Signal's
    • Cloud history is stored indefinitely
    • Your IP address and phone number can go to authorities on a court order
    free telegram.org →

    worth knowing

    The best messenger is the one your people will use. Signal on everyone's phone beats a perfect tool nobody installed. Move one group chat at a time; the family one is usually easiest.

    Turn on disappearing messages. Encryption protects messages in transit; it does nothing about the archive sitting on a stolen or seized phone. A default timer (a week, say) is cheap insurance.

    WhatsApp is better than its owner suggests, and worse than it looks. It runs the Signal protocol underneath, so content encryption is solid. But it's Meta-owned, metadata is the product, and the privacy policy reflects that.

    Back up your keys. On Matrix especially: lose your key backup passphrase and your encrypted history is gone for good. Store it in your password manager.
