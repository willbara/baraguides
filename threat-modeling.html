    last updated 2026-11-05 · what changed

    what it is

    Threat modeling means deciding what you're protecting, who you're protecting it from, and how much effort it's worth. You already do it. You lock your bike differently in a small town than outside a train station, and you don't tell a stranger what you told your doctor.

    Most privacy and security advice skips this step. It tells everyone to use Tor, self-host everything, or get off Google, as if the whole internet shared one enemy. Each tip helps some people and wastes other people's time. Some backfire, because an exotic setup can make you easier to notice. A threat model lets you read any advice, here or anywhere else, and tell whether it's for you.

    Maximum security is a phone in a lake, and nobody can live like that. Aim for the right security for your situation, at a cost you will still pay in six months.

    the three questions

    1 · what are you protecting?

    Think of your email, photos, money, location, messages, and who you talk to. Security people call these your assets. Be specific. "My privacy" is too vague to defend. "My home address staying off people-search sites" is something you can act on.

    2 · who are you protecting it from?

    It might be advertisers and data brokers (companies that collect and resell personal data), scammers, a thief with your phone, or someone you know. Security people call them adversaries. Each can do different things, and most people's real list is shorter than they fear.

    3 · what will you put up with?

    Every defense costs money, time, or daily hassle. One you quit protects nothing. Ask what inconvenience you will still tolerate in six months, and build around that.

    what this site believes

    The boring threats are the probable ones. Reused passwords, phishing messages (fake emails and texts that trick you into handing over a login), and out-of-date phones cause far more harm than spies do. Fix those before anything exotic.

    A defense you abandon protects nothing. A modest setup you keep beats a perfect one you drop in week two.

    Privacy is a spectrum, and nobody has to pass a purity test. You can use a big company's service and still protect yourself well. Every step you take counts.

    Name the problem before you buy the fix. Much of the noise in this field sells fear or products. If advice can't say who it's for, skip it, and that includes the advice on this site.

    A few people face real, targeted threats. If that's you, the answer is specialist help, and it starts on the Hardened page.

    build your model

    1. List what you'd hate to lose or leak. Write it in a note. Most lists include an email account, photos, money, private conversations, and where you are. Add one or two things specific to you, such as health records or a business idea.
    2. Think about who would really want each item. For most people the honest list is data brokers and ad networks (they want everything, passively), criminals (they want money from anyone), and sometimes one person they know. Surveillance by a government is real but rare. Designing for it first is how people burn out and quit.
    3. Guess how likely each one is, and how bad it would be. A rough guess is enough. The grid below shows where common threats land.
    4. Fix the biggest risks first, with the cheapest fix that works. Pick one defense for each. Then use it for a month before you add more.
    5. Revisit when life changes. A new job, country, relationship, or child changes your model, and so does a public role. Skim yours once a year. It takes ten minutes.

    real-world examples

    The everyday account holder

    Protecting: email, banking, and shopping accounts.

    From: bots and scammers who try passwords leaked in other breaches, plus phishing messages.

    First moves: a different generated password for every account, two-factor login (2FA, a second step such as an app code) on email and banking, and automatic updates. The Essentials setup is this list.

    The person who shares a lot online

    Protecting: your home address, daily routine, family photos, and employer.

    From: strangers, scammers who use real details to sound believable, and anyone who reads old posts later, such as an employer or landlord.

    First moves: check who can see each account, trim old posts, and keep your address off public listings. Keep public and private life on separate accounts. See Social Media and Personal Data Removers.

    The browser, shopper, and streamer

    Protecting: what you search, buy, and watch.

    From: ad networks and data brokers. The harm from each event is small, but it never stops, and the profile gets sold on.

    First moves: a tracker-blocking browser, filtered DNS, and a separate email alias for each signup.

    The shared household

    Protecting: your messages, search history, and accounts on shared devices.

    From: a partner, roommate, or relative with casual access, such as a shared tablet or a passcode they know.

    First moves: a screen lock only you know, a separate profile on each shared device, and your own accounts for private things. If someone uses that access to watch or control you, go to the next section.

    when it's bigger than this

    The basics cover the everyday threats. If any of these sound like you, your situation is bigger:

    • Someone knows things about you they shouldn't, like where you've been or what you wrote.
    • You see logins, forwarded emails, or devices you don't recognize.
    • A partner, ex, or relative monitors or controls your phone or accounts.
    • Your work makes you a target for a powerful group: journalism, activism, research, or law.
    • You have been threatened, followed, or harassed online.

    Start with the Hardened setup, which opens with a short triage: a person watching you, or an organization or state. Then get specialist help. Access Now's Digital Security Helpline, the EFF's Surveillance Self-Defense guides, and Freedom of the Press Foundation's training are free and current. For threats from someone you know, the Coalition Against Stalkerware lists vetted local resources.

    if you are in danger now Contact one of those groups before you change your devices or accounts. Sudden changes can alert the person watching you. If your safety is at risk right now, call your local emergency number.

    where to begin

    Push comes to shove, the best single thing you can do is practice good password manager hygiene. That means a unique, generated password for every account, and 2FA on your email. This stops the most common real-world attacks, and it takes an afternoon. Bitwarden is a reasonable place to begin.
